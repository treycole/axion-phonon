Which crystal vibrations can move the axion angle θ

A from-scratch explanation of the symmetry arguments: how vectors and axial vectors transform, what time reversal adds, why only non-degenerate modes couple linearly, why those modes are invisible to ordinary light, and why magnons cannot do the job in bulk.

0The short version

  1. θ multiplies \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\). That fixes how θ behaves under every symmetry: it flips sign under inversion and mirrors, and it flips sign under time reversal. Any symmetry of the crystal that flips θ forces θ = −θ, which (because θ is only defined mod 2π) means θ = 0 or π.
  2. A vibration with amplitude Q can change θ at first order only if Q flips sign under exactly the same operations that flip θ. Such a mode is necessarily non-degenerate (a 1D irrep). Degenerate modes (E, T) can only change θ at Q², Q³ or later.
  3. A mode that is infrared-active must transform like a dipole. A mode that is axion-active must transform like θ. Both at once requires θ × dipole to be a symmetric quantity, and θ × dipole transforms like a magnetization. So an axion phonon can be seen in IR absorption only if the crystal is allowed a net magnetization. Neither of our antiferromagnets is, so their axion phonons are dark.
  4. Magnetic moments are unchanged by inversion. In both materials the magnetic ions sit on inversion centres, so any uniform tilt of the spins (a magnon) keeps inversion, and inversion keeps θ at 0 or π. In bulk MnBi₂Te₄ the only way to make θ oscillate is to move the atoms.
  5. All of this was checked by a script that reads the actual DFT structures and moments, finds their magnetic symmetry groups by brute force, and counts which modes qualify. It needs no wavefunctions and runs in about one second.

1What "transforms as" means

A symmetry operation \(g\) of a crystal is a rigid motion that maps the crystal onto itself: a rotation, a reflection, an inversion, possibly combined with a translation. Its rotation part is a 3×3 orthogonal matrix \(R\). There are two kinds:

  • Proper operations have \(\det R = +1\): ordinary rotations \(C_n\) by \(360°/n\).
  • Improper operations have \(\det R = -1\): inversion \(i\) (\(R=-1\), every point \(\mathbf r\to-\mathbf r\)), mirrors \(\sigma\), and rotoinversions such as \(S_6\) (a rotation combined with inversion). Improper operations turn a right hand into a left hand.

Every physical quantity has a fixed rule for how it changes when you apply \(g\). "X transforms as a vector" just means X changes by the same rule as a vector does. The rules that matter here:

Polar vectors

A polar vector is an ordinary arrow: \(\mathbf v \to R\,\mathbf v\). Position, an atomic displacement \(\mathbf u\), the electric field \(\mathbf E\), an electric dipole \(\mathbf p\) and the polarization \(\mathbf P\) are polar. Under inversion a polar vector reverses: \(\mathbf v\to-\mathbf v\). Think of a dipole as a + charge above a − charge; inversion swaps their positions, so the arrow now points down.

Axial vectors

An axial vector (or pseudovector) is defined by a sense of rotation, like a cross product \(\mathbf a = \mathbf r\times\mathbf p\). It transforms as \(\mathbf a \to (\det R)\,R\,\mathbf a\). Angular momentum, spin, a magnetic moment \(\mathbf m\), the magnetization \(\mathbf M\) and the magnetic field \(\mathbf B\) are axial. Under inversion an axial vector does not change: both factors of the cross product flip, so their product does not. The picture is a current loop. Inversion sends each point of the loop to the opposite side and reverses its velocity, so the loop ends up circulating the same way and its moment still points up.

Time reversal

Time reversal \(T\) runs the motion backwards. It reverses velocities, currents and spins, and leaves positions and charges alone. So \(\mathbf u\), \(\mathbf E\), \(\mathbf p\) are time-even, and \(\mathbf B\), \(\mathbf M\), spins and currents are time-odd. A frozen phonon displacement is time-even: reversing time does nothing to where the atoms are.

Scalars and pseudoscalars

A number can also carry a sign rule. A true scalar (energy, mass) never changes. A pseudoscalar changes sign under improper operations: \(s\to(\det R)\,s\). The dot product of a polar and an axial vector is a pseudoscalar, because \(R\mathbf v\cdot(\det R)R\mathbf a = (\det R)\,\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a\). Both \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\) and \(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf m\) are pseudoscalars, and both are also time-odd.

2Symmetry in a magnet: primed operations

In a non-magnetic crystal a symmetry only has to map atoms onto atoms of the same kind. In a magnet it also has to map each magnetic moment onto the moment that is actually there. Some operations map every atom correctly but reverse all the moments. Those are not symmetries on their own. Time reversal also reverses all the moments, so the combination \(g' = T\cdot g\) puts them back and is a symmetry. Operations that include \(T\) are written with a prime. We record this with a sign:

\[ s(g) = +1 \text{ (unprimed)}, \qquad s(g) = -1 \text{ (primed, contains } T). \]

Time reversal by itself is never in the group of a magnet, since it would reverse every moment.

MnBi₂Te₄ (A-type antiferromagnet)

The crystal is a stack of septuple layers (SLs). The Mn moments point along \(c\), up in one SL and down in the next, so the magnetic cell contains two SLs. Some of its 24 operations:

  • Translation by one SL, called \(S\) below. It maps every atom onto an identical atom, but the moments come out reversed. So \(S\) alone is not a symmetry; \(S' = T\cdot S\) is. This is the operation usually called \(\Theta\) in the axion-insulator literature.
  • Inversion about a Mn atom. The Mn stays put and its moment is axial, so it does not change. Unprimed.
  • A twofold axis in the plane (say along \(x\)). It sends \(m_z\to -m_z\), so it appears only as the primed \(C_2'\).

Total: 24 operations, 12 of them primed.

Y₂Ir₂O₇ (all-in-all-out)

Each Ir tetrahedron has its four moments all pointing in or all pointing out, and neighbouring tetrahedra alternate. A fourfold rotation about a cubic axis turns an "all-in" tetrahedron into an "all-out" one, so it is a symmetry only with \(T\) attached: \(C_4'\). The group is m-3m′: 48 operations, 24 primed. Inversion is unprimed, because each Ir sits on an inversion centre.

3Why θ is stuck at 0 or π

The axion angle enters the electromagnetic energy of the material as

\[ \mathcal L_\theta = \frac{e^2}{2\pi h}\,\theta\;\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B . \]

θ is a property of the crystal, so it must transform like the thing it multiplies: a time-odd pseudoscalar. For an operation \(g\) with rotation part \(R\) and prime sign \(s\), that is

\[ g:\ \theta \to \chi_\theta(g)\,\theta, \qquad \chi_\theta(g) = \det R \cdot s(g) = \pm 1 . \]

Now suppose \(g\) is a symmetry of the crystal. Applying \(g\) gives back the same crystal, so its θ cannot change. If \(\chi_\theta(g)=-1\), this says \(\theta = -\theta\). Since θ is an angle defined only modulo \(2\pi\), the two solutions are \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta=\pi\). We call these θ-odd operations. One is enough to quantize θ.

Rule 1

An operation flips θ, and so quantizes it, if it is improper and unprimed (inversion, a mirror, \(S_n\)) or proper and primed (\(C_n'\), \(S'\)). An operation that is improper and primed, like a primed mirror, has \(\chi_\theta=(-1)(-1)=+1\) and does nothing to θ.

Two things follow. First, the current paper says YIO's θ is quantized "solely by inversion". That is incomplete: the primed rotations \(C_4'\) and \(C_2'\) quantize it too. Second, in MnBi₂Te₄ inversion and \(\Theta=S'\) each quantize θ independently. To unpin θ, a distortion has to remove every θ-odd operation.

4Phonons as symmetry objects

A phonon mode at Γ (the same in every unit cell) is a pattern of displacements, one small arrow per atom, scaled by an amplitude \(Q\). Apply a symmetry operation \(g\) to a crystal distorted by \(+Q\): you get the same crystal distorted by some pattern \(gQ\). Two things can happen.

  • The pattern comes back as itself or as minus itself: \(gQ = \pm Q\) for every \(g\). The mode is non-degenerate and belongs to a one-dimensional irreducible representation (irrep). Its list of signs, one per operation, is its character.
  • The operation turns the pattern into a different pattern. Then that pattern must have the same frequency, because the crystal cannot tell them apart. The modes come as a degenerate set of 2 (labelled E) or 3 (labelled T), and each \(g\) acts on the set as a small matrix \(D(g)\) that mixes them. The character is the trace of that matrix.

An irrep is a set that symmetry mixes but cannot split further. Labels: A and B are 1D, E is 2D, T is 3D. The subscript g (gerade) or u (ungerade) is the sign under inversion: u modes reverse under inversion, like a polar vector does. An irrep often appears several times, once per inequivalent way of building it from different atoms. Those are the "copies". Symmetry decides the irrep; the force constants decide how the copies mix into the actual normal modes.

Phonons and time reversal

Displacements are time-even, so the \(T\) inside a primed operation does nothing to a phonon. A primed operation moves the atoms exactly as its unprimed geometric part does. The magnetism enters only in two places: which operations are in the group, and the sign \(\chi_\theta\) of θ.

Γ versus Z in MnBi₂Te₄

The magnetic cell holds two SLs. Every Γ pattern is either even or odd under the one-SL translation \(S\) (its geometric action, ignoring the moments):

  • (Γ), even under S: both SLs do the same thing (in phase). This is an ordinary zone-centre phonon of the single-SL crystal.
  • (Z), odd under S: the two SLs do opposite things (antiphase). In the single-SL crystal this is a zone-boundary phonon along \(c\). The doubled magnetic cell folds it back to Γ. "T₂⁻" is the label of these modes at the parent crystal's zone-boundary point.

So each pattern inside an SL comes in a pair, an in-phase and an antiphase version. The two differ only by how the neighbouring SLs push on each other, which is a weak van der Waals coupling, so their frequencies should be close. Such a pair is a Davydov pair.

5The linear rule, and why degenerate modes fail it

Expand θ in the mode amplitude: \(\theta(Q) = \theta_0 + \theta' Q + \dots\). The crystal at \(+Q\) and the crystal at \(gQ\) are the same object viewed after the operation \(g\), so their θ values are related by θ's sign rule:

\[ \theta(gQ) = \chi_\theta(g)\,\theta(Q) \pmod{2\pi}. \]

(With \(\theta_0=\pi\) this still works: \(\pi - \theta' Q \equiv -(\pi + \theta' Q)\) mod \(2\pi\).)

Non-degenerate modes

If \(gQ = \chi_Q(g)\,Q\), the linear term requires \(\theta'\chi_Q(g) = \chi_\theta(g)\,\theta'\) for every \(g\). Either \(\theta'=0\), or

\[ \chi_Q(g) = \chi_\theta(g)\quad\text{for every } g . \]

Rule 2

A mode changes θ at first order only if it flips sign under exactly the operations that flip θ, and under no others. That is a single specific 1D irrep, "θ's irrep".

Two examples of what θ's irrep looks like:

  • YIO: θ transforms like the function \(xyz\) (irrep A₂ᵤ of the cubic group). Check: a primed \(C_4\) about \(z\) sends \(xyz\to y(-x)z = -xyz\), and θ also flips under \(C_4'\). Inversion sends \(xyz\to -xyz\), and θ flips under inversion. It works for all 48 operations.
  • MnBi₂Te₄: θ transforms like \(z\) on the operations that keep each SL in place, and changes sign under the SL translation. That is A₂ᵤ(Z): a \(c\)-axis polar pattern within each SL, reversed from one SL to the next.

Degenerate modes: a worked example

Take a 3-fold degenerate T₁ᵤ mode of YIO, with components \(Q_x, Q_y, Q_z\). These transform exactly like the components of a polar vector. The most general linear term is

\[ \theta - \theta_0 = a\,Q_x + b\,Q_y + c\,Q_z . \]
  • The threefold axis along [111] is unprimed and proper, so θ is unchanged. It cycles \(Q_x\to Q_y\to Q_z\to Q_x\). Invariance forces \(a=b=c\).
  • The twofold axis along \(z\) is unprimed and proper, so θ is unchanged again. It sends \((Q_x,Q_y,Q_z)\to(-Q_x,-Q_y,Q_z)\). Invariance forces \(a=b=0\).
  • Together: \(a=b=c=0\). No linear term.

The general reason: the coefficients \((a,b,c)\) would single out one direction inside the degenerate set that symmetry leaves alone (up to sign). An irreducible set has no such direction; that is what irreducible means. So no degenerate irrep can ever couple linearly, in any crystal.

At higher order the story changes. The product \(Q_xQ_yQ_z\) transforms like \(xyz\), which is exactly θ's irrep in YIO. So T₁ᵤ modes are allowed to change θ, but only as \(Q^3\).

Breaking all the quantizing symmetries is not enough

One might think: if a distortion removes every θ-odd operation, θ moves linearly. The T₁ᵤ case shows otherwise. I froze a T₁ᵤ distortion of the YIO structure along three directions and recomputed the symmetry group:

6Counting with characters

Checking every mode by hand is impractical for 22 or 14 atoms. Character theory does the bookkeeping. For any set of objects that the group mixes among themselves (all 42 displacement coordinates, the three components of a vector, the spins), with character \(\chi_D(g) = \operatorname{tr} D(g)\), the number of independent combinations that transform exactly like θ is

\[ n_\theta(D) = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G} \chi_\theta(g)\,\chi_D(g) . \]

This is the standard orthogonality of characters. Each term is \(+1\) when the object agrees with θ under \(g\) and \(-1\) when it disagrees; only a perfect match over the whole group survives the average.

7Why axion phonons are dark to light

What light can see

  • Infrared absorption needs the vibration to carry an oscillating electric dipole. The mode must transform like a component of a polar vector (\(x\), \(y\) or \(z\)).
  • Raman scattering needs the vibration to modulate the polarizability \(\alpha_{ij}\), a symmetric tensor built like \(x^2, xy, \dots\). Such a tensor is unchanged by inversion, so only g modes can be Raman-active in a crystal with inversion.

The argument

Suppose one mode is both axion-active and IR-active along \(z\). Then θ and \(z\) transform the same way, which means the product \(\theta\cdot z\) is unchanged by every symmetry. Now look at what kind of object \(\theta\cdot z\) is: a time-odd pseudoscalar times a time-even polar vector. That is a time-odd axial vector, the same kind of object as a magnetization. Under every operation, \(\theta\cdot z\) changes exactly as \(M_z\) does (the last column of the table above is \(\chi_\theta\) times the \(z\) column). So

\[ n(\text{axion and IR along } z) = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_g \chi_\theta(g)\,\chi_z(g) = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_g \chi_{M_z}(g) = n(\text{net } M_z \text{ allowed}). \]

Rule 3 (the dark-mode rule)

An axion-active phonon can absorb infrared light polarized along a direction only if the magnetic symmetry allows a net magnetization along that direction.

If the group contains unprimed inversion, θ is odd under it while Raman tensors are even, so an axion-active phonon is never Raman-active.

Y₂Ir₂O₇ (AIAO) and MnBi₂Te₄ (A-type AFM) both forbid a net magnetization and both contain unprimed inversion. So in both materials the axion-active phonons are silent in linear IR and in Raman. This is a theorem about the symmetry, not a statement about weak intensities.

A picture for MnBi₂Te₄

A useful way to see it: count the time-odd pseudoscalar \(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf m\) in each SL, where \(\mathbf p\) is the dipole the vibration creates in that SL and \(\mathbf m\) is the SL's moment. θ responds to the sum. Light responds to the total dipole \(\sum\mathbf p\).

The ferromagnetic control

To test Rule 3, I ran the script on the same structure with both Mn moments pointing up (net \(M_z\) allowed). Prediction: the axion activity should move to the in-phase modes, and they should be IR-active. Result: A₂ᵤ(Γ) becomes axion-active and IR-active, and A₂ᵤ(Z) stops being axion-active. Of the four A₂ᵤ(Γ) copies, one is the uniform acoustic translation, which cannot change θ, so three optical modes remain.

Consequences for the paper:

  • A phonon that changes θ and can be driven directly by light requires a centrosymmetric ferromagnetic axion insulator. Candidate: MnBi₈Te₁₃, which is reported to be ferromagnetic (its axion-insulator status still needs a literature check). Bulk FM MnBi₂Te₄ is, as far as I know, predicted to be a Weyl semimetal, where θ is not defined. Both points are still to verify.
  • In AFM MnBi₂Te₄, a magnetic field along \(c\) induces a small \(M_z\). That lowers the symmetry so that A₂ᵤ(Z) and A₂ᵤ(Γ) are no longer distinct irreps and can mix. The dark mode then borrows a dipole proportional to \(M_z\), so its IR absorption grows as \(B^2\), for light polarized along \(c\), close in frequency to its IR-active twin. That is a concrete experimental signature.

8Why magnons cannot move θ in the bulk

A magnon at \(q=0\) is a small change \(\delta\mathbf m_s\) of each magnetic moment, the same in every magnetic cell, with the atoms held still. The argument takes three steps.

  1. Each magnetic ion sits on an inversion centre. In Y₂Ir₂O₇ each Ir does; in MnBi₂Te₄ each Mn does. The script finds an unprimed inversion that fixes every magnetic site in each material.
  2. Inversion does not change moments. Moments are axial vectors (Section 1). So inversion maps a site to itself and its moment \(\mathbf m_s+\delta\mathbf m_s\) to itself, whatever \(\delta\mathbf m_s\) is. Every uniform spin configuration of these crystals is inversion-symmetric.
  3. Inversion is θ-odd (\(\chi_\theta(i)=-1\)). So θ stays at 0 or π for every such configuration: not just to first order, but exactly.

This covers uniform magnons, field-induced canting, a spin flop and any rotation of the ordered moments. Phonons escape the argument because a displacement is polar: inversion reverses it (or swaps the displacements of partner atoms), so a vibration can break inversion. For the pyrochlore iridates the statement that every \(q=0\) magnetic order keeps inversion is known (Wan et al., PRB 83, 205101, 2011). For bulk MnBi₂Te₄ it means the dynamical axion has to be a lattice vibration.

Why the 2025 magnon experiment is not a contradiction

Qiu et al. (Nature 641, 62, 2025) observed an axion-like magnon in an even-layer MnBi₂Te₄ film. In a film with an even number of SLs the centre of inversion sits between the two middle layers, and inversion maps each layer onto one with the opposite moment. So the film has \(PT\) symmetry (inversion combined with time reversal), not inversion itself. A magnon that makes the top and bottom layers inequivalent breaks \(PT\) and can move θ. That mechanism relies on the film's finite thickness, so it should weaken toward the bulk, where inversion protects θ from every magnon. Whether their supplement already states the bulk no-go is still to check.

How the script works

calculations/diagnostics/2026-10-14_symmetry_selection_rules/selection_rules.py, pure NumPy, about one second.

  1. Read the cell, atomic positions and moments from the QE inputs and outputs (YIO base 210527; MBT base 210628; the mode SCF inputs).
  2. Find the magnetic group by brute force: try every integer matrix that preserves the lattice metric, every translation that maps the first atom onto an atom of its kind, and keep those that map all atoms onto atoms of the same kind. Mark an operation primed if it reverses every moment, and drop it if it does neither.
  3. Build the 3N×3N displacement matrices \(D(g)\), split them into irreps (eigenspaces of a random symmetric matrix averaged over the group), and label each irrep by its characters.
  4. Apply \(n_\theta(D) = \frac1{|G|}\sum\chi_\theta\chi_D\) to each irrep (linear coupling), to its symmetric powers \(\mathrm{Sym}^k\) for \(k\le 4\) (the order at which θ first appears), to a polar vector (IR), to a symmetric rank-2 tensor (Raman), to \(M\), and to the spin fluctuations (magnons).
  5. Recompute the group of each displaced structure and check whether any θ-odd operation survives.

The derivation and tables also live in notes/theory/axion_selection_rules.md.

What this does not cover

  • Only linear coupling and \(q=0\) (uniform within the magnetic cell). Two-phonon terms and mixed phonon–magnon terms (\(Q\,\delta m\)) are not analysed.
  • "θ first appears at Q³" means symmetry allows it, not that it is large.
  • The labels come from the input structures, so they would inherit any symmetry the inputs lacked. Both bases have their full groups (48 and 24 operations), and every displaced structure has the expected subgroup.
  • Symmetry says which modes can move θ, never by how much. The sizes come from the Wannier calculation: done for YIO, pending for MnBi₂Te₄ (8×8×8 NSCFs finish around 17 October).

10Glossary

Proper / improper
Rotation part with \(\det R=+1\) (rotations) or \(-1\) (inversion, mirrors, rotoinversions).
Polar vector
Transforms as \(R\mathbf v\); reverses under inversion. Displacement, E, dipole.
Axial vector
Transforms as \((\det R)R\mathbf a\); unchanged by inversion. Spin, magnetic moment, B.
Pseudoscalar
A single number that flips under improper operations. E·B, p·m, θ.
Time-even / time-odd
Unchanged / reversed by time reversal. Positions and E are even; spins, currents and B are odd.
Primed operation
A spatial operation combined with time reversal, \(g' = Tg\). Needed in magnets when \(g\) alone would reverse the moments.
θ-odd operation
One with \(\chi_\theta = \det R\cdot s = -1\): improper and unprimed, or proper and primed. One such symmetry pins θ to 0 or π.
Irrep
Irreducible representation: a set of modes (1, 2 or 3 of them) that symmetry mixes but cannot split further. Labels A, B (1D), E (2D), T (3D).
Character
The trace of the matrix by which an operation acts on an irrep. For a 1D irrep, just its sign ±1.
g / u

How many independent displacement patterns share an irrep. The real normal modes mix the copies.
Γ / Z labels (MBT)
Even / odd under the translation by one septuple layer: in-phase or antiphase between neighbouring layers.
Davydov pair
The in-phase and antiphase versions of the same intra-layer pattern, split by the weak interlayer coupling.
Dark mode
A mode that is neither IR- nor Raman-active, so linear optics cannot excite or detect it.
